## 1. Data motivation

This project tests whether a simple monthly top-10 momentum strategy, picking from the 100 highest-volume Nasdaq names, beats buy-and-hold on the S&P 500 (SPY) over about 30 years.<!-- P1 notebook cell 0; P3 notebook cell 0; current P3 README.md opening paragraph -->

That question is close to a choice retail traders actually face, which is why I picked it. People who buy individual stocks often do very little research, and they lean toward names that trade heavily and have done well lately. If a simple rule chasing those names clearly beat holding the index, that would be surprising. If it doesn't, that backs up the usual advice: stick with the index.<!-- P1 notebook cell 1, paraphrased -->

P1 through P3 ask the same question; what shifted, once the data were in hand, was the method.<!-- P1 notebook cell 0; P2 notebook cell 0; P3 notebook cell 0 and current P3 README.md --> There's no volume column in the Nasdaq Symbol Directory, so "Nasdaq's 100 highest-volume names" can't be read from Source 1 by itself.<!-- P3 notebook cell 5 --> That's why P3 pulled prices for every Nasdaq Global Select common stock in good standing on the snapshot date, and the P4 notebook rebuilds the analysis from that saved pull. Every month it ranks those names by average daily dollar volume and buys the top 10 of the 100 highest-dollar-volume names.<!-- P3 notebook cells 5, 20, and 29; P4 executed notebook cells 0 and 6 --> Dollar volume (close times volume) is used since Yahoo's close and volume already come split-adjusted; ranking by share count would favor stocks that have split.<!-- P3 notebook cell 33; P4 executed notebook cell 0; docs/data_dictionary.md section 2 --> The directory is also a current list, not a history, so companies delisted later never enter the pool.<!-- P3 notebook cells 20 and 31 --> None of this rewrites the original question, but these method constraints shape what any later number can be said to mean, and Sections 6 and 7 return to them.

## 5. Integration & verification

The join was specified in the notebook before the merge took place: the left table is `prices_monthly` (one row per ticker per month); the right table is `directory` (one row per symbol, test issues already eliminated); the key is `symbol`, after Yahoo's "-" has been mapped to ".". The intended join is many-to-one: many months for a single symbol. The join is a left join so every price row is retained, the directory simply adds its name, exchange, and flags; unmatched price rows would be retained, flagged, and would fail validation. An inner join was rejected because it would silently delete rows with simple spelling differences (for example BRK-B versus BRK.B). A right or outer join would introduce thousands of directory symbols with no prices. pandas `validate="many_to_one"` is used so that a duplicate directory symbol would raise rather than fan out rows.<!-- P3 notebook cell 23 -->

The P4 run printed 231,997 left rows, 13,216 directory symbols, 231,997 join rows, 231,997 matched rows on both sides, and 0 left-only or right-only rows.<!-- P4 executed notebook cell 12 output; results/findings.csv, inputs: n_joined_rows, n_unmatched_price_rows --> Of the directory symbols, 542 of 13,216 contain a "." or "$" that Yahoo would spell differently; following the mapping, no priced ticker was left unmatched.<!-- the 542 is a count of directory symbols containing a period or dollar sign, computed from data/processed/directory.csv and not printed in the executed notebook; the 0 unmatched is from P4 executed notebook cell 12 output -->

## 6. Findings

The rule, as run in the P4 notebook, is: each month, rank stocks (never SPY) by their average daily dollar volume over the past 12 months, take the top 100, buy the 10 of those with the highest 12-month return, equally weighted, and hold for the next month. No trading costs, taxes, or slippage are subtracted.<!-- P4 executed notebook cell 15 markdown and cell 16 run_backtest(); README.md "What the project is and what it found" --> Over February 1997 to September 2026, 356 months or 29.7 years, that rule turned $1 into $736.22, a compound annual growth rate of 24.9%. Over the same months, buy-and-hold SPY turned $1 into $16.18, a compound annual growth rate of 9.8%.<!-- results/findings.csv, scenario baseline: n_months, final_wealth_strategy, final_wealth_spy, cagr_strategy, cagr_spy; P4 executed notebook cell 16 output --> The rule's monthly return beat SPY's in 60.1% of those months, which is 214 of 356.<!-- results/findings.csv, baseline, share_months_strategy_beats_spy = 0.601123595506; 214 is that share times 356 months --> This is the headline run, and it keeps all 21 flagged monthly returns. The P3 saved run gave the same headline figures, and the P4 notebook states that the data, the filter, the join and the rule did not change since P3.<!-- P4 executed notebook cell 22 markdown; logs/verification_notes.md --> `figures/growth_baseline.png` plots both paths on a log scale. The rule's path goes up steeply around 2000, dips, then climbs through the remainder of the period.<!-- figures/growth_baseline.png -->

The higher return came with much more risk. The rule's annualized volatility, the standard deviation of its monthly returns times the square root of 12, was 36.7%, against 15.3% for SPY.<!-- results/findings.csv, baseline, ann_vol_strategy = 0.367067233093 and ann_vol_spy = 0.152984721498 --> Its monthly returns swung more than twice as much as SPY's. This project does not calculate a risk-adjusted metric, so it does not assert that the rule outperformed after accounting for that risk.

How much the answer might have moved, if the months had played out differently, is shown by a bootstrap. The notebook drew 2,000 samples, with replacement, of the 356 pairs of months' returns, using seed 4052026. The 5th to 95th percentile range of the rule's compound annual growth rate was 11.6% to 38.6%. For SPY it was 4.7% to 15.0%. Those two ranges overlap, so the pairing matters more: resampling the months in pairs, the rule's growth rate minus SPY's was between +4.2 and +26.4 percentage points (5th to 95th percentile), and it finished ahead of SPY in 98.95% of the samples.<!-- results/findings.csv, scenario bootstrap_baseline: bootstrap_seed, bootstrap_reps, cagr_strategy_p05 = 0.115601288638, cagr_strategy_p95 = 0.386275559748, cagr_spy_p05 = 0.047077113322, cagr_spy_p95 = 0.1500655551, cagr_gap_paired_bootstrap_p05 = 0.0421162553051, cagr_gap_paired_bootstrap_p95 = 0.263843462847, cagr_gap_share_resamples_positive = 0.9895 --> The rule's ending wealth, for $1, ran from $25.67 to $16,151.19 (5th to 95th percentile) and SPY's from $3.91 to $63.30 in the same band.<!-- results/findings.csv, bootstrap_baseline: final_wealth_strategy_p05, final_wealth_strategy_p95, final_wealth_spy_p05, final_wealth_spy_p95 --> The bootstrap takes the months as independent draws, but real monthly returns are not independent, so these ranges are indicative, not rigorous confidence intervals.<!-- P4 executed notebook cell 17 markdown; results/findings.csv note on n_months bootstrap_reps: months treated as independent -->

The result is not carried by one stretch of time. Split into three subperiods, the rule's compound annual growth rate was ahead of SPY's in all three. From February 1997 to December 2006 (119 months) it was 16.1% versus 7.7%. From January 2007 to December 2016 (120 months) it was 21.8% versus 6.9%. From January 2017 to September 2026 (117 months) it was 38.0% versus 15.2%.<!-- results/findings.csv, scenarios subperiod_1997-02_to_2006-12, subperiod_2007-01_to_2016-12, subperiod_2017-01_to_2026-09: n_months, cagr_strategy, cagr_spy --> The rule's growth rate was highest in the latest period, and `figures/subperiod_cagr.png` shows the three pairs next to the whole-sample pair. Proportion of months where the rule beat SPY was 54.6%, 68.3%, and 57.3% across the three periods.<!-- results/findings.csv, three subperiod scenarios, share_months_strategy_beats_spy --> Looking at three periods is very rough, and these later periods are also no guarantee for the future.

The 21 flagged returns were checked directly, because the P3 notebook did not know how much they mattered. The notebook reruns the same rule on copies of the price path, and leaves the headline table alone. Removing all 21 jumps raised the rule's final wealth to $822.40, a growth rate of 25.4%. Capping all 21 at +300% gave $764.30, or 25.1%. Removing only the CHRD jump of November 2020 made no difference: $736.22 and 24.9%, the same as the headline.<!-- results/findings.csv, scenarios exclude_flagged, cap_300, exclude_chrd_2020_11: final_wealth_strategy, cagr_strategy; docs/cleaning_log.csv row 24 for the method --> SPY's numbers are the same in all these scenarios. So the CHRD jump does not drive the result. None of the 21 flagged returns was earned by a held portfolio.<!-- results/findings.csv, inputs, n_flagged_jumps_held_in_portfolio = 0 --> The October 2020 portfolio, whose return was earned in November 2020, held NVAX, ZM, TSLA, NXH, PTON, MRNA, DKNG, FSLY, ZS, and DOCU, and not CHRD.<!-- P4 executed notebook cell 16 output; data/processed/backtest_full.csv, row for month 2020-10 --> CHRD was held in four other portfolios, chosen from December 2016 through March 2017, long before the jump.<!-- data/processed/backtest_full.csv, rows 2016-12 to 2017-03 --> The flags do not inflate the headline: removing them raises it by $86.18, and capping them raises it by $28.08. Since the held portfolios never earned a flagged return, the difference can only come through the rankings, because a jump sits inside a ticker's 12-month momentum for a year after it happens. Why removing the jumps helps is suspected and not tested. The suspicion is that the jumps made some names look like good momentum candidates and pulled the rule into stocks that then handed the gains back.<!-- difference arithmetic: 822.40 - 736.22 = 86.18 and 764.30 - 736.22 = 28.08, from results/findings.csv; mechanism from P4 executed notebook cell 17 markdown and README.md "Other checks" --> `figures/growth_sensitivity.png` plots the four paths, and on a log scale they are nearly indistinguishable.<!-- figures/growth_sensitivity.png -->

What this table shows, under this construction, is that a backtest on the current Global Select common-stock pool, using Yahoo's cached chart data, generated a much higher terminal value and compound annual growth rate than SPY, with much larger volatility, and that this holds up to the flagged returns, the bootstrap, and the three subperiods. Our question in this project was whether the rule beats buy-and-hold on the S&P 500. On return, in this table, it does. That is a description of this table, not a claim that the rule was tradable in real time or that each return input is reliable.

What remains suspected, or at least unproven, is any firmer conclusion that "momentum works" or that a retail trader would have earned 24.9% a year. Several documented limits sit between the table and that claim, and Section 7 takes up the missing data in detail. First, 6 of the 1,037 planned tickers never entered the price table (INDV, MGPI, NATH, QRVO, SLP, and WBD).<!-- P4 executed notebook cell 10 output; results/findings.csv, inputs, n_skipped_tickers = 6 --> Second, just 316 of 1,031 parsed tickers have data back to January 1996, so early months draw from a much smaller sample than the overall 1,036-name pool.<!-- computed from data/processed/prices_monthly.csv (rebuilt by the notebook): tickers whose first month is 1996-01; the count is not printed in the executed notebook --> Third, the 21 flagged jumps remain unexplained, including CHRD at +30,990.9% in November 2020, a jump the cached chart does not match to a split. The sensitivity runs show the headline is not sensitive to them, but they do not reveal what the jumps were.<!-- results/findings.csv, inputs, chrd_2020_11_return = 309.909076444; P4 executed notebook cell 14 output --> Fourth, the backtest uses equal weights and does not deduct trading costs, taxes, or slippage, with the rule rebalancing 10 names each month.<!-- P4 executed notebook cell 15 markdown; README.md "Known limits" --> Fifth, and most crucial for interpretation, the pool is drawn from a directory of names that still exist on the snapshot date, so failed and delisted companies are excluded. The 24.9% versus 9.8% comparison is therefore best read as an upper bound on what the rule would have earned, and the extent of that bias is not assessed here.<!-- README.md "What the project is and what it found" and "Known limits" --> The P3 notebook itself said the comparison "seems like a definite yes, but it isn't proof yet," and stated "the real answer needs those flagged returns reviewed, not just a bigger sample."<!-- P3 notebook cell 31 (DSA405_002_FA26_P3_rdhoover.ipynb, submitted for P3, not in this repo) --> P4 conducted the sensitivity portion of that review. It did not determine the cause of any flag, nor did it add data for the missing firms.

## 7. What is missing

The directory is a snapshot of what is listed and traded on Nasdaq systems as of 17 September 2026. Companies that were Nasdaq-listed or Nasdaq-traded and were later delisted, acquired, or otherwise removed are not included.<!-- P2 notebook cell 26; P3 notebook cells 20 and 31 --> Because the full-run pool is "Global Select common stocks listed today,"<!-- P3 notebook cell 32 output --> a firm that failed is not in the 1,036-name list and would never be selected in any month, including months when it was still trading. If these failed companies had worse returns than the survivors, the 24.9% compound rate would be biased upward. That bias is not quantified in the P3 and P4 materials, which is why Section 6 reads the headline as an upper bound.

That snapshot filter also excludes, by design, all names that are not Nasdaq-listed Global Select common stocks in normal standing: other Nasdaq tiers, other exchanges, ETFs and related funds, preferred shares and other non-common products, issuers that are deficient, delinquent, or otherwise not coded Normal, and test symbols.<!-- P3 notebook cell 6 --> SPY is left out of the stock pool and used as a benchmark only.<!-- P3 notebook cell 29 --> BRK.A is marked in the file as the one security not traded on Nasdaq and is omitted from the P2 analysis extract; it is not in the P3 price pool.<!-- P2 cleaning log decision 18; P3 notebook cell 6 --> Those exclusions mean the result is not a conclusion about Nasdaq as a whole, or about ETFs, or about smaller Nasdaq tiers.

Six tickers passed the pool filter but are still missing from the price table because their cached responses had no `timestamp` path: INDV, MGPI, NATH, QRVO, SLP, and WBD.<!-- P4 executed notebook cell 10 output; data/processed/fetch_failures.csv --> Any month in which one of these would have ranked in the top 100 by dollar volume or the top 10 by momentum is a month the backtest could not consider it. The notebook does not count how often that would have happened.

Coverage across the 1,031 parsed tickers is uneven. Only 316 have prices back to January 1996.<!-- computed from data/processed/prices_monthly.csv, first month 1996-01; not printed in the executed notebook --> Amazon, for example, starts in May 1997 in this cache.<!-- P4 executed notebook cell 10 output, AMZN first date 1997-05-15 --> Late-listed survivors enter the pool when Yahoo's history of the name begins, which is simply another way to look at names that survived, not at the market as it was then.

Null values for price or volume were removed on FISV (1 day) and PSKY (40 days).<!-- P4 executed notebook cell 10 output --> These holes are tiny compared to delisting, but they are isolated gaps in two series.

One thing this data can never tell us, no matter how we scrub it, is how the same monthly rule would have worked in a universe that included the companies later removed from Nasdaq's directory. There is no historical directory in this project, since Yahoo's cache was only requested for names on the current list. We cannot recover that counterfactual from these files.<!-- P3 notebook cells 20 and 31 -->
